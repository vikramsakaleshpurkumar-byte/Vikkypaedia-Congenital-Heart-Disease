<details><summary>Which ECG relationship establishes complete atrioventricular dissociation?</summary>

Atrial and ventricular activation are independent. In complete heart block, P waves and QRS complexes occur independently, rather than maintaining a fixed conducted relationship.

<details><summary>Why do intermittent cannon A waves occur in complete heart block?</summary>

Right atrial contraction against a closed tricuspid valve. Independent atrial and ventricular activity can bring atrial contraction against the closed tricuspid valve, abruptly amplifying the venous A wave.

</details>

<details><summary>Why can the first heart sound vary from beat to beat during complete heart block?</summary>

The changing atrial–ventricular timing alters valve position at ventricular systole. Because atria and ventricles beat independently, the PR relationship changes and first-sound intensity can vary widely.

</details>

<details><summary>Can a normal-looking chest radiograph rule out congenital complete heart block?</summary>

Assess the atrial–ventricular relationship on a standard ECG. The radiographic appearance can be normal; the source emphasizes the standard ECG for establishing complete heart block.

</details>

<details><summary>Why are narrow QRS complexes common in isolated congenital complete heart block?</summary>

The usual subsidiary pacemaker is above the bifurcation of the His bundle, preserving a normal ventricular activation sequence. The source also discusses exceptions, so QRS width is interpreted with the whole ECG.
