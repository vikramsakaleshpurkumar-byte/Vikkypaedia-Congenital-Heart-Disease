<details><summary>Which pattern is characteristic of a Still murmur?</summary>

A Still murmur. The typical Still murmur is vibratory or buzzing, midsystolic and most prominent between the apex and lower left sternal edge in the supine child.

<details><summary>Where are transient neonatal branch pulmonary artery systolic murmurs typically distributed?</summary>

Transient disparity between the pulmonary trunk and its branch sizes. The neonatal pulmonary trunk–branch size disparity and branch geometry can produce widely transmitted systolic turbulence; the murmur alone does not distinguish transient physiology from fixed branch stenosis.

<details><summary>How can positional behavior support recognition of a venous hum?</summary>

A cervical venous hum. Recumbency, neutral neck position and reduced jugular flow can diminish or abolish the venous hum.

<details><summary>What response to shoulder hyperextension supports a supraclavicular arterial systolic murmur?</summary>

Flow vibrations near the origins of the major brachiocephalic arteries. The supraclavicular arterial murmur is systolic, maximal above the clavicles and may diminish or disappear with shoulder hyperextension.

<details><summary>Why does finding a ventricular band not establish the cause of every Still murmur?</summary>

Band prevalence persists across ages while Still murmur prevalence falls toward adolescence. The mismatch between age-related murmur prevalence and band prevalence, and the source’s explicit uncertainty, prevent treating the association as a settled universal mechanism.

</details>

The source describes a rare transient neonatal ductal diastolic murmur in the first few days of life. Persistent or otherwise unexplained diastolic sounds should not be dismissed by applying an innocent-murmur label.
