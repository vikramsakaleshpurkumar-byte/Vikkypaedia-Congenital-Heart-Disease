<details><summary>How does systemic venous blood reach the ventricle in tricuspid atresia?</summary>

Right-to-left flow across an interatrial communication. Systemic venous blood must cross from right atrium to left atrium and pass through the patent left AV valve.

</details>

<details><summary>Why can spontaneous VSD closure be harmful in tricuspid atresia with normally related great arteries?</summary>

Increasing subpulmonary obstruction limits lung flow. With normally related arteries, the ventricular communication provides access to the pulmonary circuit; narrowing can produce acquired pulmonary atresia.

PDF p. 418
</details>

<details><summary>What can a restrictive VSD obstruct when tricuspid atresia is accompanied by transposed great arteries?</summary>

Subaortic obstruction to systemic output. In this arrangement left ventricular blood must traverse the defect to reach the transposed aorta; restriction creates subaortic stenosis.

PDF p. 418
</details>

<details><summary>What ECG pattern supports the common non-transposed form with a restrictive ventricular defect?</summary>

Left-axis deviation with adult-like precordial progression and right atrial P waves. The source summary describes left-axis deviation, counterclockwise depolarization, adult precordial progression and right atrial P waves in this common arrangement.

PDF p. 430
</details>

<details><summary>What does echocardiography with color flow and Doppler establish in tricuspid atresia?</summary>

Echocardiography with color flow and Doppler. Echo identifies the echodense absent connection, chamber sizes, great arterial relationships and shunt or obstruction physiology.

PDF p. 429
</details>

<details><summary>Why can mild cyanosis coexist with heart failure in tricuspid atresia?</summary>

A nonrestrictive ventricular defect and low pulmonary vascular resistance can cause excessive pulmonary flow and left ventricular volume overload, reducing cyanosis at the price of heart failure.

</details>
