<details><summary>Where are the usual aortic and pulmonary openings of a patent ductus?</summary>

Patent ductus arteriosus. The normal ductal insertion is near the left pulmonary artery, with its aortic end distal to the left subclavian artery.

</details>

<details><summary>How does a left-to-right ductal shunt load the left ventricle?</summary>

Increased pulmonary venous return imposes left ventricular volume load. Pulmonary recirculation returns to the left atrium and ventricle, increasing left-heart volume load.

PDF p. 351
</details>

<details><summary>How does the classic uncomplicated ductal murmur relate to the second sound?</summary>

Classic continuous ductal murmur. The classic ductal sound envelops the second heart sound with continuous systolic-to-diastolic flow.

PDF p. 358
</details>

<details><summary>Why can a reversed ductal shunt cause cyanotic toes with a pink right hand?</summary>

Pulmonary arterial blood entering the descending aorta through a ductus distal to the right-arm supply. The ductal entry usually delivers desaturated blood to the lower body after the proximal arch vessels have branched.

PDF p. 355
</details>

<details><summary>Why does reversed flow through an aortopulmonary window usually lack differential cyanosis?</summary>

Desaturated blood enters proximal to systemic arch distribution. A window delivers blood into the ascending aorta, unlike the usual distal ductal insertion.

PDF p. 367
</details>

<details><summary>Why can a clinically silent ductus remain patent?</summary>

Jet direction and pressure-flow conditions can prevent an audible murmur; Doppler imaging can identify patency despite absent auscultatory signs.

PDF p. 351
</details>
