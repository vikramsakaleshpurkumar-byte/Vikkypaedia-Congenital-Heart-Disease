<details><summary>Where does a noncoronary sinus aneurysm most often rupture?</summary>

Right atrium. The noncoronary sinus is anatomically related to the right atrium; right coronary sinus aneurysms commonly enter the right ventricle or sometimes the right atrium.

PDF p. 396
</details>

<details><summary>Why can a sudden large sinus rupture cause rapid congestive failure?</summary>

The heart has little time to adapt to the acute shunt volume burden. Size, rapidity and receiving chamber determine the burden; sudden substantial recirculation can overwhelm adaptation.

PDF p. 396
</details>

<details><summary>What should a new continuous murmur with acute symptoms suggest in someone with a ventricular septal defect?</summary>

Rupture of a coexisting sinus of Valsalva aneurysm. The source highlights this change as a clue to an aortic sinus aneurysm rupturing into the right side of the heart.

PDF p. 399
</details>

<details><summary>How may a sinus rupture murmur differ from a classic ductal murmur?</summary>

A ruptured aortic sinus communication is plausible. Sinus rupture murmurs can be continuous without the characteristic second-sound peak of the classic ductal murmur.

PDF p. 402
</details>

<details><summary>How can an aortic sinus aneurysm cause atrioventricular block?</summary>

Injury to the AV node or His bundle near the septal base. Ruptured or unruptured aneurysms penetrating the septal base can disrupt nearby conduction tissue.

PDF p. 403
</details>

<details><summary>Can an unruptured sinus aneurysm produce a to-and-fro murmur?</summary>

Yes. Flow into and out of the intact pouch can cause murmurs in both phases; color Doppler assessment is needed to distinguish this from an actual perforation.

PDF p. 403
</details>
