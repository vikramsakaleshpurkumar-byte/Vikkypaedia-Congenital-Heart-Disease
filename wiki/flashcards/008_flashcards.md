<details><summary>Which pulse comparison is useful for detecting coarctation?</summary>

Compare upper-extremity and femoral pulses and blood pressures. Upper–lower extremity pulse and pressure differences are hallmarks; brachial–femoral comparison is particularly practical in infants.

PDF p. 120
</details>

<details><summary>How does the proximal aorta contribute to coarctation-associated systolic hypertension?</summary>

A stiff, poorly distensible precoarctation aorta. Increased collagen and reduced smooth muscle stiffen the proximal aorta, contributing to systolic hypertension and baroreceptor resetting.

PDF p. 116
</details>

<details><summary>Why does absent rib notching not exclude coarctation in infancy?</summary>

Notching usually develops later through enlarged posterior intercostal collateral arteries. Collateral posterior intercostal arteries produce notching, which seldom appears before age six years.

PDF p. 126
</details>

<details><summary>When can a neonate with severe coarctation become acutely symptomatic?</summary>

Closure of the ductus arteriosus. The source specifically associates acute neonatal symptoms in severe coarctation with ductal closure.

PDF p. 116
</details>

<details><summary>How can femoral pulses be maintained initially in an infant with interrupted aortic arch?</summary>

Pulmonary trunk through a patent ductus into the descending aorta. Pulmonary trunk–ductus–descending aortic continuity can supply the lower body; diminishing femoral pulses can signal ductal constriction.

PDF p. 131
</details>

<details><summary>Where may a coarctation murmur be heard in an infant when anterior auscultation is unrevealing?</summary>

Over the posterior thoracic spine near the coarctation; murmurs may diminish or disappear with heart failure.

PDF p. 124
</details>
