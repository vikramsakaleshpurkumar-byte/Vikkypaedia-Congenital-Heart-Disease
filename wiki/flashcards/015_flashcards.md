<details><summary>Which malalignment links the principal anatomy of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary>

Anterior and cephalad deviation of the infundibular septum. Outlet septal malalignment creates the ventricular defect, narrows right ventricular outflow and contributes to aortic override.

</details>

<details><summary>What chiefly determines shunt direction in classic tetralogy with a nonrestrictive defect?</summary>

More right-to-left flow into the aorta and less pulmonary flow. Relative outflow and systemic resistances determine the balance; severe outflow obstruction diverts blood toward the aorta.

PDF p. 301
</details>

<details><summary>Why can the pulmonary stenotic murmur soften during a hypoxic spell?</summary>

A sharp decline in forward pulmonary blood flow. Less blood traverses the stenotic pulmonary outflow, so the murmur shortens and softens despite worsening hypoxemia.

PDF p. 309
</details>

<details><summary>How can squatting improve oxygenation in an unrepaired tetralogy circulation?</summary>

Increased systemic resistance and reduced return of desaturated leg blood. Squatting can redirect right ventricular flow toward the lungs and curtail desaturated lower-extremity venous return.

PDF p. 307
</details>

<details><summary>Why is right ventricular systolic pressure usually systemic in classic tetralogy?</summary>

The ventricle can decompress through the communication into the systemic outlet. The nonrestrictive defect imposes a systemic pressure ceiling; partial occlusion or true restriction can remove that protection.

PDF p. 302
</details>

<details><summary>Why can pulmonary atresia in this anatomy coexist with only mild cyanosis?</summary>

Abundant systemic-to-pulmonary collateral flow can supply effective pulmonary gas exchange despite absent forward right ventricular pulmonary flow.

</details>
