<details><summary>What makes a ventricular septal defect restrictive?</summary>

Restrictive defect. Restriction at the defect limits pressure transmission and shunt flow, leaving a systolic pressure difference.

</details>

<details><summary>Why can a sizable ventricular left-to-right shunt become symptomatic after the newborn period?</summary>

Falling pulmonary vascular resistance increases left-to-right flow. The neonatal fall in pulmonary resistance permits a larger shunt and pulmonary venous return, imposing left ventricular volume load.

PDF p. 270
</details>

<details><summary>How can pulmonary vascular disease reverse a nonrestrictive ventricular shunt?</summary>

Right-to-left shunting from pulmonary vascular disease. With equal ventricular systolic pressures, relative vascular resistances govern flow; suprasystemic pulmonary resistance favors reversal.

PDF p. 268
</details>

<details><summary>Why may the murmur shorten and soften as pulmonary vascular resistance rises?</summary>

Reduced left-to-right flow can reflect worsening pulmonary vascular disease. As pulmonary resistance approaches systemic, the left-to-right murmur can shorten, soften and eventually disappear with reversal.

PDF p. 275
</details>

<details><summary>Which valve abnormality should be sought when a ventricular defect is accompanied by aortic regurgitation?</summary>

Aortic cusp prolapse and regurgitation. Perimembranous and subarterial defects can be associated with aortic cusp prolapse; regurgitant volume can explain disproportionate left ventricular enlargement.

PDF p. 288
</details>

<details><summary>How can perimembranous ventricular defects close spontaneously?</summary>

Adherence of septal tricuspid leaflet tissue to the defect margins is a common mechanism and can be associated with a membranous septal aneurysm.

</details>
