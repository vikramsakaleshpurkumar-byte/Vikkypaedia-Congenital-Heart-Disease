<details><summary>What defines a parachute mitral valve?</summary>

Parachute mitral valve. A single papillary muscle receiving all chordae defines the parachute arrangement and can restrict interchordal flow.

<details><summary>Which obstructive lesions form the classic Shone complex?</summary>

Supravalvular mitral ring, parachute mitral valve, subaortic stenosis and coarctation. The classic four lesions are left-sided inflow/outflow obstructions; not all need be present or functionally significant.

</details>

<details><summary>Why may congenital mitral stenosis lack a loud S1 and opening snap?</summary>

The abnormal leaflet apparatus may not make the brisk opening and closing movements required for those sounds. The characteristic brisk motion of a mobile anterior leaflet is not a feature of the congenital lesion described.

</details>

<details><summary>Where is the typical cor-triatriatum membrane relative to the left atrial appendage?</summary>

Cor triatriatum. A membrane proximal to the appendage and valve distinguishes typical cor triatriatum from a supravalvular ring.

<details><summary>Why can congenital pulmonary vein stenosis cause nonuniform pulmonary congestion without left atrial enlargement?</summary>

Unequal stenosis of individual pulmonary veins proximal to the left atrium. Individual veins can have different severities of narrowing, producing regional congestion while the downstream left atrium remains normal-sized.

</details>

<details><summary>How does the partition in typical cor triatriatum divide the left atrium?</summary>

A proximal high-pressure accessory chamber receives the pulmonary veins; the distal chamber contains the left atrial appendage and fossa ovalis.
