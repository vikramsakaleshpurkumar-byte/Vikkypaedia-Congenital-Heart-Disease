Truncus arteriosus. A single great artery and single semilunar valve supply the three circulations.

<details><summary>How does type 1 truncus differ from a pattern with separate pulmonary branch ostia?</summary>

Type 1 truncus. Type 1 has a short main pulmonary artery from the trunk; separate branch ostia constitute the other grouped pattern.

PDF p. 472
</details>

<details><summary>Why can cyanosis diminish while heart failure worsens in an infant with truncus arteriosus?</summary>

Falling pulmonary resistance increases lung flow and ventricular volume load. More pulmonary flow improves systemic oxygenation but imposes volume overload and may cause severe heart failure.

PDF p. 475
</details>

<details><summary>Why can truncal valve regurgitation produce biventricular failure?</summary>

Regurgitant flow is received by both ventricles and adds to their burden. The biventricular truncus permits regurgitant volume to load both ventricles, adding to existing hemodynamic stress.

PDF p. 475
</details>

<details><summary>What associated syndrome should be considered with truncus, hypocalcemia and deficient cellular immunity?</summary>

DiGeorge syndrome. The chapter describes DiGeorge-related thymic and parathyroid deficiency with truncus arteriosus.

PDF p. 479
</details>

<details><summary>What imaging observations define truncal valve dysfunction?</summary>

Color flow imaging assesses regurgitation, and continuous-wave Doppler assesses stenosis, alongside valve morphology and the pulmonary branching anatomy.
