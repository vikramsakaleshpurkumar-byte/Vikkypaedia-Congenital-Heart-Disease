## Authored MCQ

A young person has upper-extremity hypertension. Which bedside examination most directly investigates an aortic obstruction?

A. Compare upper-extremity and femoral pulses and blood pressures
B. Use carotid pulse strength alone
C. Use the absence of an anterior murmur as the decisive test
D. Examine only the two radial pulses

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Compare upper-extremity and femoral pulses and blood pressures

Upper–lower extremity pulse and pressure differences are hallmarks; brachial–femoral comparison is particularly practical in infants.

## Authored MCQ

An adolescent with coarctation has a disproportionate systolic pressure rise during exercise. Which additional vascular feature helps explain it?

A. Exposure of renal arteries to the same proximal hydraulic pressure in every case
B. Complete independence of arterial wall properties from systolic pressure
C. Uniformly increased proximal aortic compliance
D. A stiff, poorly distensible precoarctation aorta

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

A stiff, poorly distensible precoarctation aorta

Increased collagen and reduced smooth muscle stiffen the proximal aorta, contributing to systolic hypertension and baroreceptor resetting.

</details>

## Authored MCQ

A symptomatic infant has no rib notching on chest radiography. Which interpretation is best?

A. Notching is an obligatory neonatal sign before symptoms appear
B. Notching usually develops later through enlarged posterior intercostal collateral arteries
C. The sign reflects the anterior ribs alone
D. Absence of notching excludes an important narrowing

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Notching usually develops later through enlarged posterior intercostal collateral arteries

Collateral posterior intercostal arteries produce notching, which seldom appears before age six years.

## Authored MCQ

An infant has an interrupted arch but initially palpable femoral pulses. What connection can maintain distal perfusion?

A. Interatrial flow directly entering the femoral arteries
B. A normal brachial pulse proving normal arch continuity
C. Pulmonary trunk through a patent ductus into the descending aorta
D. Direct continuity across the absent arch segment

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Pulmonary trunk through a patent ductus into the descending aorta

Pulmonary trunk–ductus–descending aortic continuity can supply the lower body; diminishing femoral pulses can signal ductal constriction.

</details>
